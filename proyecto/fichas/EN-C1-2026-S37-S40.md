## Week 37 · 7–13 Sep · EN-F01 · Giving and Asking for Opinions
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Building an opinion that survives being questioned*

**Topic:** T01 Work & Career · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~45 min + 15 min

**Description.** At C1 the problem is no longer *how* to say what you think but how to say it so that it holds. A C1 opinion has three parts a B1 opinion doesn't: a stated position, a reason that isn't a restatement of the position, and an acknowledged limit. Most learners produce the first, skip the second and never reach the third — which is why their opinions sound firm but empty. The other half of the skill is eliciting: pulling a real opinion out of someone who is being polite or vague.

**Reference box — opinion moves**

| Move | What it does | Weak → Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Framing | announces a judgement is coming | *I'd say* → *My sense is* → *My position is* → *I'd argue* |
| Grounding | supplies the reason, not a restatement | *because* → *the reason being* → *what makes me say that is* |
| Distinguishing | splits a question others treat as one | *I'd separate two things* / *I'd distinguish between X and Y* |
| Limiting | names where the claim stops | *Where I'd stop short is* / *I wouldn't extend it to* |
| Sourcing | marks evidence vs. hunch | *That's my instinct, not my evidence* |
| Eliciting | extracts a real answer | *What's your read?* → *Where do you land?* → *Is that a hunch or have you seen it?* |
| Deferring | concedes the other knows more | *You'd know better than me* / *I'm outside my lane* |

**Examples.**
- *My position is that hybrid work only fails where management was already weak — the office was covering for the problem, not solving it.*
- *I'd distinguish between two things: whether it works, and whether people like it.*
- *Where I'd stop short is claiming this applies to every role.*
- *Is that a hunch, or have you actually seen it happen?*

**Notes.**
- *I think* isn't weak, but it's cheap. Rotate through the framing column instead of repeating it.
- *I'd distinguish between X and Y* is the highest-value move here: it signals you've thought about the question rather than reacted to it.
- Naming the limit of your own claim strengthens it. Counter-intuitive, but reliably true in English argument.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish can hedge inside the verb (*creo que sea* vs. *creo que es*); English can't. *I think* is only a frame, never a hedge, so English needs a second device stacked on top of it to soften anything.

**Common mistakes.**
- Circular reasoning: *I think remote work is good because working remotely is beneficial.*
- *In my humble opinion* — reads as sarcastic to native ears. Avoid entirely.
- *According to me* — not English. Use *in my view*.
- Over-reliance on *for me*, which frames a judgement as a mere preference.

## Week 38 · 14–20 Sep · EN-V07 · Discourse Markers and Linking
*Occurrence 1 of 4 — Signposting a long spoken turn*

**Topic:** T11 Current Events & News · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~45 min + 20 min

**Description.** Discourse markers are the difference between speech that is understood and speech that is followed. At C1 the job isn't adding *however* to writing — it's managing a spoken turn of two or three minutes so the listener always knows where they are: what's coming, what's a digression, what's the main point returning. Spoken and written markers are largely different sets, and using written ones in speech is the commonest C1 register error.

**Reference box — markers by function and register**

| Function | Spoken | Written |
|---|---|---|
| Announcing structure | *There are two things going on here* / *Let me take those in turn* | *This raises two questions* |
| Adding | *Also* / *On top of that* / *And another thing* | *Moreover* / *Furthermore* |
| Contrasting | *That said* / *Then again* / *Mind you* | *However* / *Nevertheless* |
| Conceding | *Granted* / *Fair enough, but…* / *True up to a point* | *Admittedly* / *While it is true that…* |
| Digressing | *Just as an aside…* | *(parenthesis)* |
| Returning | *Anyway, the point is…* | *Returning to…* |
| Reformulating | *Or to put it another way* / *What I mean is* | *That is to say* |
| Concluding | *So on balance* / *The upshot is* | *In sum* / *On this evidence* |

**Examples.**
- *There are two things going on here. Let me take those in turn.*
- *Granted, the numbers moved. Then again, they moved from a very low base.*
- *Just as an aside — and this isn't the main point — the timing was odd. Anyway, the upshot is that nobody checked.*

**Notes.**
- *Actually* marks correction or surprise, not emphasis. *Actually, it was cheaper* implies you expected otherwise.
- *Basically* is a filler; *essentially* is a marker. Not interchangeable.
- *Anyway* is the standard return-from-digression signal in speech. Learners rarely use it; natives use it constantly.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish *en realidad* maps onto *actually* only about half the time — often it's just *well*. *De hecho* is *in fact*. Treating them as one-to-one equivalents is where most misuse starts.

**Common mistakes.**
- *In the other hand* — it's *on the other hand*.
- *Moreover* and *furthermore* in speech: instantly bookish.
- *At the end* meaning *finally* — use *in the end*.
- Stacking: *So, basically, at the end of the day, essentially…*

## Week 39 · 21–27 Sep · EN-F02 · Diplomatically Disagreeing
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Attacking the assumption, not the person*

**Topic:** T03 Technology & AI · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~45 min + 15 min

**Description.** English separates the strength of a disagreement from its social temperature more sharply than Spanish does. You can reject an idea completely and still sound collaborative, and the mechanism is almost entirely formulaic — which means it is learnable in a week. The core move: attack the idea, never the person, and always leave the other position somewhere to stand.

**Reference box — disagreement by temperature**

| # | Formula | What it targets | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Help me understand how that would work in practice.* | the mechanism | you suspect it collapses and want them to find it |
| 2 | *I see it differently, and here's why.* | the conclusion | default professional register |
| 3 | *I'd challenge the assumption underneath that.* | the premise | the logic is fine, the starting point isn't |
| 4 | *I hear you, but I don't think that follows.* | the inference | premise granted, conclusion refused |
| 5 | *I'm going to push back on that.* | the whole claim | direct but still not hostile; very standard |
| 6 | *I think that's straightforwardly wrong.* | the whole claim | reserve for when you mean it |

**Examples.**
- *I'd challenge the assumption underneath that rather than the conclusion — you're assuming the failure mode is technical.*
- *Help me understand how that would work with a 200-millisecond budget.*
- *I hear you, but I don't think the second half follows from the first.*

**Notes.**
- *I'm afraid I disagree* is textbook English and sounds slightly antique in speech. Fine in writing.
- Attacking the *assumption* rather than the *conclusion* is the highest-value C1 move: it lets the other person retreat without losing face.
- *With respect* is a warning sign in British English — it almost always precedes a demolition. Use knowingly or not at all.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish carries disagreement largely in intonation and in *pero*; English carries it lexically. A literal English rendering of a normally forceful Spanish objection lands roughly two levels harsher than intended.

**Common mistakes.**
- *I am not agree* — a fossilised Spanish-speaker error worth naming once and killing.
- *You are wrong* — grammatically fine, socially expensive, rare in professional English.
- Softening until the disagreement disappears. If nobody noticed you disagreed, you didn't.
- *No, but…* as an opener. Reverse it: *Yes, and here's the difficulty…*

## Week 40 · 28 Sep–4 Oct · Skill Review Week

**Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 10 min of speech or 500 words · **Time:** ~60 min

**What this week is for.** Three weeks of separate practice have given you three separate habits. Nothing so far has forced them to run at the same time, which is what real conversation demands. This week has no new material — the difficulty is entirely in the combination.

**Reference box — what's under review**
