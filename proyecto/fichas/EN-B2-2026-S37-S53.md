## Week 37 · 7–13 Sep · EN-G03 · Present Perfect vs. Past Simple
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — The tense choice that Spanish speakers get wrong for years*

**Topic:** T02 Personal Finance & Banking · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~35 min + 15 min

**Description.** The rule is short: past simple for finished time, present perfect for time that still includes now. The difficulty is not the rule but the habit. Spanish uses the compound past far more freely, so *I have bought a house last year* feels natural and is wrong. This week the aim is to make the choice consciously, every time, until it stops needing thought.

**Reference box — which tense, and why**

| Situation | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Finished time stated | past simple | *I opened the account **in March**.* |
| Finished time understood | past simple | *I called the bank.* (this morning, over) |
| Time still open | present perfect | *I've saved a lot **this year**.* |
| Life experience, no time given | present perfect | *I've never had a loan.* |
| Past action, present result | present perfect | *I've lost my card.* (I still don't have it) |
| Started in the past, still true | present perfect (+ *for* / *since*) | *I've banked with them **for** ten years.* |
| Asking when | **always** past simple | *When **did** you open it?* |

*With **yesterday**, **last week**, **in 2019** and **ago**, only the past simple is possible. With **just**, **already**, **yet** and **so far**, normally the present perfect.*

**Examples.**
- *I've been with the same bank since 2015, but I changed my account type last year.*
- *Have you ever paid for something in instalments? — Yes, I bought a laptop that way.*
- *I've already transferred the money.*

**Notes.**
- **When** always takes the past simple, even about very recent events. *When have you arrived* is one of the most recognisable learner errors.
- *For* and *since* with the present perfect mean the situation continues. *I lived there for ten years* means you left.
- British English uses the present perfect more than American English. Both are correct; be consistent.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish uses *he comprado* for finished past time in much of Spain, and the simple past almost always in Latin America. Neither maps onto English: the English rule is about whether the **time period** is finished, not about how recent the event was.

**Common mistakes.**
- *I have bought it last year* — finished time needs the past simple.
- *When have you started?* — *when* always takes the past simple.
- *I am here since Monday* — English needs *I have been here since Monday*.
- Using the present perfect for a narrative sequence. Stories go in the past simple.

## Week 38 · 14–20 Sep · EN-V07 · Discourse Markers and Linking
*Occurrence 1 of 4 — Joining ideas so the listener can follow*

**Topic:** T09 Education & Lifelong Learning · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~35 min + 15 min

**Description.** At B2 the sentences are usually correct but they sit next to each other without a visible connection. Discourse markers are the words that show the reader how one idea relates to the next: adding, contrasting, giving a reason, drawing a conclusion. Learning ten and using them well is worth more than knowing fifty.

**Reference box — markers by function**

| Function | Common in speech | Common in writing |
|---|---|---|
| Adding | *also*, *on top of that*, *what's more* | *moreover*, *in addition* |
| Contrasting | *but*, *though*, *that said* | *however*, *nevertheless* |
| Giving a reason | *because*, *since* | *as*, *given that* |
| Giving a result | *so*, *that's why* | *therefore*, *consequently* |
| Giving an example | *like*, *for instance* | *for example*, *such as* |
| Conceding | *OK, but…*, *fair enough* | *although*, *admittedly* |
| Reformulating | *I mean*, *in other words* | *that is to say* |
| Concluding | *so in the end*, *all in all* | *in conclusion*, *overall* |

*The left column is not "worse" English: it is the right register for speaking. Using the right column out loud sounds like reading an essay.*

**Examples.**
- *I started the course late. That said, I caught up within a month.*
- *The classes were online, so I could study after work.*
- *It's a long course. On top of that, it's expensive.*

**Notes.**
- *Though* at the end of a sentence is very common in speech: *It was hard. I enjoyed it, though.*
- *So* and *because* answer opposite questions. *Because* gives the reason, *so* gives the result. Learners often swap them.
- One marker per sentence is enough. Two in a row cancel each other out.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish tolerates long sentences joined with commas; English prefers shorter sentences joined with markers. Translating a Spanish paragraph directly produces one very long English sentence that a reader has to unpick.

**Common mistakes.**
- *In the other hand* — it is *on the other hand*.
- *Also* at the start of a spoken sentence sounds abrupt; *and also* or *on top of that* is more natural.
- *Although* and *but* together: *Although it was hard, but I finished.* Only one is needed.
- *For example* followed by a whole clause with no example in it.

## Week 39 · 21–27 Sep · EN-G11 · Passive Voice
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Choosing the passive on purpose, not by accident*

**Topic:** T06 Environment & Sustainability · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~40 min + 15 min

**Description.** The passive is not a more formal way of saying the same thing. It changes what the sentence is about. *They closed the plant* is about them; *The plant was closed* is about the plant. Use it when the doer is unknown, obvious, unimportant, or deliberately not mentioned — which is why it is everywhere in news and in technical writing.

**Reference box — forms and when to use it**

| Tense | Passive form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present simple | *is / are* + past participle | *The waste **is collected** weekly.* |
| Past simple | *was / were* + pp | *The law **was passed** in 2019.* |
| Present perfect | *has / have been* + pp | *Targets **have been missed**.* |
| Future | *will be* + pp | *It **will be replaced**.* |
| Modal | *modal + be* + pp | *It **can be recycled**.* |
| Continuous | *is / are being* + pp | *The plant **is being closed**.* |

**Use the passive when:** the doer is unknown (*the samples were mislabelled*), obvious (*he was arrested*), irrelevant (*the results were published*), or when the object is the real topic of the sentence.
**Say who did it with *by*** — but only when it matters: *The report was written **by the ministry***.

**Examples.**
- *Single-use plastics were banned last year.*
- *The targets have been missed three times.*
- *Most of it can be recycled, but it isn't.*

**Notes.**
- Only verbs with an object can be made passive. *Happen*, *arrive*, *occur* and *die* have no passive form.
- The passive without *by* is the normal case. Adding *by* every time makes writing heavy.
- Don't write everything in the passive. Two or three in a paragraph is plenty.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish usually avoids the passive and uses *se* instead: *se recogen los residuos*. English has no equivalent of that *se*, so where Spanish uses it, English normally takes the passive. That is why a Spanish speaker under-uses the passive in English and it sounds oddly personal.

**Common mistakes.**
- Forgetting *be*: *The law passed in 2019* means the law itself did the passing.
- Wrong participle: *was writed*, *was teached*.
- Making an intransitive verb passive: *The accident was happened*.
- Using the passive with no reason, which makes the text vague.

## Week 41 · 5–11 Oct · EN-F11 · Summarising
*Occurrence 1 of 4 — Saying the important part first*

**Topic:** T01 Work & Career · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~35 min + 15 min

**Description.** Most people summarise by telling the story in order. That is retelling, not summarising. A summary starts with the conclusion and then gives the two or three things that support it. At B2 the hard part is deciding what to leave out — and being willing to leave it out.

**Reference box — the shape of a summary**

| Step | What to do | Language |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Say the main point first | *The main thing is…* / *In short,…* |
| 2 | Give two or three supports | *First… Second… And…* |
| 3 | Say what you skipped | *I'm leaving out the details of…* |
| 4 | Add a limit if there is one | *Though I only saw part of it.* |
| 5 | Keep your opinion separate | *That's what happened. What I think is…* |

**Examples.**
- *In short, the project is late and it's a staffing problem, not a technical one.*
- *Three things came up. The important one is the third.*
- *That's what the report says. Whether I agree is another matter.*

**Notes.**
- Say how long you need before you start: *Give me a minute and I'll explain.* People listen differently when they know the length.
- Saying what you left out makes the summary more trustworthy, not less.
- Numbers help: *three things* tells the listener when you will finish.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish often builds up to the conclusion and puts it at the end. English expects it at the start. Keeping the Spanish shape makes an English summary feel like the speaker is avoiding the point.

**Common mistakes.**
- Retelling in chronological order.
- *Resume* meaning *summarise*. In English *resume* means to start again. The verb is *to summarise*, the noun is *a summary*.
- Including every detail because leaving one out feels dishonest.
- Mixing what happened with what you think about it.

**Topic vocabulary — T01 Work & Career (B2)**

| Item | Type | Note | ES | DE | FR | IT | PT |

## Week 42 · 12–18 Oct · EN-G25 · Past Perfect and Narrative Time
*Occurrence 1 of 2 — Saying which past thing happened first*

**Topic:** T11 Current Events & News · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~40 min + 15 min

**Description.** The past perfect has one job: to show that one past event happened before another past event. *When I arrived, he left* and *When I arrived, he had left* describe two different situations. Without it, a story told out of order becomes impossible to follow. It is a B1 structure that stays difficult through B2 because it is easy to avoid.

**Reference box — past perfect at a glance**

| Point | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Form | *had* + past participle, all persons | *had gone*, *had been*, *had seen* |
| Core use | earlier of two past events | *The police arrived after the crowd **had left**.* |
| With *when* | order matters | *When I got there, it **had** already **started**.* |
| With *by the time* | almost always past perfect | *By the time we knew, it **had spread**.* |
| Reported speech | past simple becomes past perfect | *"I saw it" → He said he **had seen** it.* |
| Third conditional | in the *if* clause | *If they **had checked**, this wouldn't have happened.* |
| **Not needed** when | *before* or *after* already show the order | *He left before I arrived.* |

**Examples.**
- *By the time the story was published, the company had already denied it.*
- *Nobody knew the reporter had been there.*
- *She said she hadn't seen the email.*

**Notes.**
- *Before* and *after* already make the order clear, so the past perfect is optional there. With *when*, it changes the meaning, so it is not optional.
- Contractions are normal and worth practising out loud: *I'd*, *he'd*, *they'd* — and *'d* is also *would*, so context decides.
- English uses the past perfect more than Spanish uses the pluperfect. When in doubt about order, use it.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish has the same structure (*había llegado*) but uses it less and often relies on context instead. Since the mechanism already exists in Spanish, this is a habit problem, not a comprehension problem.

**Common mistakes.**
- Using it for a simple sequence with no reason: *I had got up and I had had breakfast.* One past simple after another is enough.
- *When I arrived he left* when you meant he was already gone.
- Wrong participle: *had went*, *had ate*.
- Forgetting it in reported speech.

## Week 43 · 19–25 Oct · EN-V03 · Advanced Phrasal Verbs
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Using them instead of avoiding them*

**Topic:** T05 Travel & Culture Shock · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~35 min + 15 min

**Description.** Phrasal verbs are the part of English that looks optional and is not. There is almost always a one-word alternative, and it almost always sounds more formal than the situation needs. Saying *I tolerated it* instead of *I put up with it* is correct English and the wrong register. At B2 the goal is to stop avoiding them.

**Reference box — how they split**

| Type | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Separable, with a noun | either order works | *pick up the keys* / *pick the keys up* |
| Separable, with a pronoun | **must** split | *pick them up*, never *pick up them* |
| Inseparable | never splits | *get over an illness* |
| Three-part | never splits | *put up with*, *look forward to*, *get on with* |
| No object | nothing follows | *break down*, *set off*, *check in* |

*If you only remember one rule: **a pronoun always goes in the middle.***

**Examples.**
- *We set off early and checked in without any trouble.*
- *It took me months to get used to it.*
- *The car broke down and we had to sort it out ourselves.*

**Notes.**
- Many have a formal one-word twin: *find out / discover*, *put off / postpone*, *look into / investigate*. Both are correct; the phrasal one is normal in speech.
- The same phrasal verb can mean very different things. *Pick up* can mean collect, learn, improve or answer the phone. Context decides.
- Three-part verbs are where learners most often drop the middle word. *Put up with*, not *put with*.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish has no phrasal verbs, so the first word that comes to mind is always the Latin-origin one — which exists in English but sits a register higher. That mismatch is why correct English can still sound stiff.

**Common mistakes.**
- Pronoun after the particle: *turn on it*, *pick up it*.
- Dropping the middle word of a three-part verb.
- *Discuss about* — *discuss* takes no preposition, though *talk about* does.
- Choosing the formal verb every time.

## Week 45 · 2–8 Nov · EN-F15 · Managing a Conversation
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Keeping your turn while you think*

**Topic:** T04 Health & Wellbeing · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 10-min conversation or 250 words · **Time:** ~30 min + 15 min

**Description.** Most courses teach you to build sentences. Almost none teach you what to do when you are halfway through one and the word won't come. That gap is why people who know a lot of English still go quiet in conversation. The moves are few and they are learnable by heart.

**Reference box — four moves**

| Move | What it does | Say |
|---|---|---|
| Take the turn | get in without waiting for silence | *Can I say something?* / *Sorry, can I just add —* |
| Hold the turn | keep it while you think | *Hold on, let me think.* / *What's the word… give me a second.* |
| Repair | fix a sentence you've lost | *Sorry, let me say that again.* / *That's not what I meant.* |
| Give the turn | hand it back on purpose | *What do you think?* / *Sorry, go on.* |

*Filled pauses — *well*, *so*, *I mean*, *right* — keep the turn. Silence gives it away.*

**Examples.**
- *Hold on, let me think how to say this.*
- *Sorry, let me say that again — I meant the opposite.*
- *What's the word… symptoms. That's it.*

**Notes.**
- Saying out loud that you are looking for a word is normal. Native speakers do it constantly.
- Never apologise for your English mid-conversation. It moves the other person's attention onto your level instead of your point. Say *give me a second* instead.
- Asking someone to repeat is a skill, not a failure: *Sorry, could you say that again?*

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish conversation allows more overlap and more interruption, and silence is less uncomfortable. English compensates by making the taking of a turn explicit: where Spanish just starts talking, English announces first.

**Common mistakes.**
- *Sorry, my English is bad.* Never say this.
- Going silent while thinking, which hands the turn to the other person.
- Abandoning a sentence halfway instead of repairing it out loud.
- Waiting for a clear gap that never comes.

## Week 46 · 9–15 Nov · EN-G13 · Reported Speech
*Occurrence 1 of 2 — Saying what someone else said*

**Topic:** T12 Hobbies & Free Time · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~40 min + 15 min

**Description.** Reporting what someone said means moving three things at once: the tense goes back, the pronouns change, and the time and place words change. Most learners handle the tense and forget the other two, which produces sentences that are grammatically fine and factually confusing.

**Reference box — what moves**

| What | Direct | Reported |
|---|---|---|
| Present simple | *"I play"* | *he played* |
| Present continuous | *"I'm playing"* | *he was playing* |
| Past simple | *"I played"* | *he had played* |
| Present perfect | *"I've played"* | *he had played* |
| *will* | *"I'll play"* | *he would play* |
| *can* | *"I can play"* | *he could play* |
| **Pronouns** | *"**I** like **my** team"* | *he liked **his** team* |
| **Time** | *today / tomorrow / now* | *that day / the next day / then* |
| **Place** | *here / this* | *there / that* |

*Questions lose the inversion: "Do you play?" → **he asked whether I played**, not *he asked did I play*.*
*Orders use the infinitive: "Stop!" → **he told me to stop**.*

**Examples.**
- *She said she had been playing for years.*
- *He asked whether I wanted to join them the next day.*
- *They told us not to bring anything.*

**Notes.**
- *Say* takes no person: *he said that…*. *Tell* needs one: *he told **me** that…*. Mixing them is the most common error of all.
- The tense does not move back if what was said is still true: *He said he lives in Bern* is fine if he still does.
- *Whether* and *if* both work for yes/no questions. *Whether* is slightly more formal.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish shifts tenses in reported speech too, so the mechanism is familiar. What is not familiar is the *say* / *tell* split: Spanish uses *decir* for both, and that single difference produces more errors here than the whole tense system.

**Common mistakes.**
- *He told that…* or *He said me that…*
- Keeping the question word order: *She asked where was the club.*
- Forgetting the pronoun shift, so the sentence reports the wrong person.
- Forgetting *not* in negative orders: *He told me to don't come.*

## Week 47 · 16–22 Nov · EN-F16 · Complaining and Resolving Problems
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Getting it fixed without a fight*

**Topic:** T07 Housing & City Life · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~35 min + 15 min

**Description.** An effective complaint in English has a shape: say what happened, say what it caused, say what you want. Most unsuccessful complaints skip the third part and only express annoyance. The tone is also formulaic — English keeps the pressure on the problem and off the person, and that is what makes it work.

**Reference box — the three parts, and how hard to push**

| Part | Function | Language |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · The facts | what happened, no adjectives | *The heating hasn't worked since Monday.* |
| 2 · The effect | why it matters | *We haven't been able to use the flat.* |
| 3 · The request | what you want, specifically | *Could you send someone this week?* |

| Strength | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Soft | *I was wondering if you could…* |
| Normal | *Could you please…* / *I'd appreciate it if…* |
| Firm | *I'd like this sorted out by Friday.* |
| Formal escalation | *I'm afraid I'll have to take this further.* |

**Examples.**
- *The heating hasn't worked since Monday, so we've been using an electric heater. Could you send someone this week?*
- *I was wondering if you could look into it.*
- *I'd like this sorted out by Friday, please.*

**Notes.**
- Ask for something specific with a date. *Please fix it soon* is easy to ignore; *by Friday* is not.
- *I'd appreciate it if you could…* is the most useful all-purpose formula: polite and clearly a request.
- Stay on the problem, not the person. *You never answer* invites a defence; *I haven't had a reply* states a fact.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Direct requests are more acceptable in Spanish than in English, so a literal translation of a normal Spanish complaint sounds aggressive. The English version needs a softener — *could*, *would*, *I'd appreciate it* — even when the complaint itself is firm.

**Common mistakes.**
- Complaining without asking for anything.
- *I want you to fix it* — grammatically fine, socially expensive.
- Apologising so much that the complaint disappears.
- *You always* and *you never*, which turn a problem into an accusation.

## Week 49 · 30 Nov–6 Dec · EN-G07 · Third Conditional
*Occurrence 1 of 2 — Talking about what didn't happen*

**Topic:** T03 Technology & AI · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~40 min + 15 min

**Description.** The third conditional talks about a past that did not happen: *If we had backed it up, we wouldn't have lost it.* Both halves are unreal. The form looks heavy — *had* plus participle on one side, *would have* plus participle on the other — but it is fixed, and once the shape is automatic the meaning is simple. It is the natural language of regret and of blame, which makes it useful well beyond grammar practice.

**Reference box — the three conditionals side by side**

| Type | *If* clause | Main clause | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | present simple | *will* + verb | real future possibility |
| Second | past simple | *would* + verb | unreal present or future |
| **Third** | **past perfect** | ***would have* + participle** | **unreal past** |

*If we **update** it, it **will work**.* → possible
*If we **updated** it, it **would work**.* → we aren't going to
*If we **had updated** it, it **would have worked**.* → we didn't, and it didn't

**Variations worth knowing:**
- *could have* and *might have* instead of *would have*, for possibility rather than certainty.
- Without *if*, by inversion: *Had we known, we would have acted.* Formal, and common in writing.
- The *if* clause can go second, with no comma: *We wouldn't have lost it if we had backed it up.*

**Examples.**
- *If they had tested it properly, the bug wouldn't have reached users.*
- *We might have noticed earlier if the alerts had been set up.*
- *Had we known about the update, we would have waited.*

**Notes.**
- Never *would have* in the *if* clause. *If I would have known* is wrong, though it is common in speech in some varieties.
- Contractions matter for listening: *If I'd known, I'd have said.* Both *'d* are different words — *had* and *would*.
- *Should have* is the close relative: *We should have tested it* is the same regret without the conditional.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish has an exact equivalent (*si lo hubiéramos sabido, habríamos esperado*), so the meaning transfers directly. What does not transfer is the *would have* in the *if* clause: Spanish allows *si hubiera* in both halves in some regions, and that habit produces the commonest error here.

**Common mistakes.**
- *If I would have known…*
- *If I had known, I would known* — dropping the *have*.
- Mixing the second and third: *If I had studied, I would be a doctor* is actually correct as a mixed conditional, but usually it is written by accident rather than on purpose.
- Wrong participles: *would have went*, *had wrote*.

## Week 50 · 7–13 Dec · EN-F12 · Persuading and Negotiating
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Making it easy to say yes*

**Topic:** T14 Consumption & Commerce · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~35 min + 15 min

**Description.** Persuading in English is less about pushing and more about framing: showing how your proposal helps the other person, offering something in exchange, and making the next step small. The grammar behind it is the conditional you already know — *if you could…, I'd be able to…* — so the work here is knowing which move to use, not learning new structures.

**Reference box — the moves**

| Move | What it does | Say |
|---|---|---|
| Open high | sets the reference point | *I was hoping for around…* |
| Conditional offer | gives nothing unless they move | *If you could…, I'd be able to…* |
| Ask about their side | finds the real need | *What would work for you?* |
| Offer a trial | lowers the risk | *What if we tried it for a month?* |
| Say no and offer something | keeps the door open | *I can't do that, but I could…* |
| Give a reason | raises agreement measurably | *…because otherwise I'd have to…* |

**Examples.**
- *If you could include delivery, I'd be happy to take two.*
- *What would work for you?*
- *I can't go that high, but I could pay today.*

**Notes.**
- A conditional offer concedes nothing until the other side moves. That is why it is the safest move to start with.
- *What would work for you?* is worth memorising. It gets more information than any statement.
- Giving a reason increases agreement even when the reason is weak. *Because* does real work in English.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Bargaining in Spanish often starts near the real number; English expects a starting figure that leaves room. Opening with your real minimum is read as your maximum, and the negotiation then goes downward from there.

**Common mistakes.**
- Saying *I want* repeatedly. Use *I'm looking for*, *what would help is*.
- Giving something away without asking for anything back.
- Repeating the same argument louder instead of changing the frame.
- Saying only *no*, with no alternative, which ends the conversation.

## Week 51 · 14–20 Dec · EN-G20 · Unreal Past: *I wish*, *if only*, *it's time*
*Occurrence 1 of 2 — Past tense that isn't about the past*

**Topic:** T08 Relationships & Family · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~40 min + 15 min

**Description.** After *I wish*, *if only* and *it's time*, English uses a past tense to talk about the present. *I wish I knew* is about now, not about yesterday. It looks like a mistake and it is the rule. This is the structure of regret, frustration and wanting things to be different — which makes it one of the most emotionally useful things at B2.

**Reference box — which tense after which expression**

| Expression | Tense | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *I wish* + past simple | past form | present regret | *I wish I **had** more time.* |
| *I wish* + past perfect | past perfect | past regret | *I wish I **had called** her.* |
| *I wish* + *would* | *would* + verb | annoyance at someone else | *I wish he **would listen**.* |
| *If only* + past / past perfect | same as *wish* | stronger version | *If only I **had known**.* |
| *It's time* + past simple | past form | it should already have happened | *It's time we **talked**.* |
| *I'd rather* + past simple | past form | preference about someone else | *I'd rather you **didn't**.* |
| *as if / as though* + past | past form | unreal comparison | *He talks as if he **knew** everything.* |

***Were*, not *was*,** in careful English: *I wish I **were** there.* *Was* is very common in speech and accepted, but *were* is the safe choice in writing.

**Examples.**
- *I wish we lived closer to my parents.*
- *If only I had said something at the time.*
- *I wish he would stop apologising.*
- *It's time we talked about it properly.*

**Notes.**
- *I wish* + *would* cannot be about yourself. *I wish I would stop* is wrong; you control that, so it is *I wish I could stop*.
- *It's time* with an infinitive is different and simpler: *It's time to go* is neutral. *It's time we went* carries a mild reproach.
- *I'd rather* takes a bare infinitive for yourself (*I'd rather go*) and a past tense for someone else (*I'd rather you went*).

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish uses the subjunctive here — *ojalá tuviera más tiempo* — and the subjunctive is a distinct verb form. English has no separate subjunctive left, so it borrows the past tense. The logic is the same: an unreal form for an unreal situation.

**Common mistakes.**
- *I wish I have more time* — the present tense breaks the structure.
- *I wish he will listen* — after *wish*, *would*, never *will*.
- *I wish I would stop smoking* — use *could* for yourself.
- *It's time we talk* — needs the past form, *talked*.

## Week 53 · 28 Dec–3 Jan · EN-F13 · Hedging and Softening
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Saying it carefully without losing the point*

**Topic:** T10 Food & Cooking · **Type:** constrained production · **Evidence:** 250 words or 4 min · **Time:** ~35 min + 15 min

**Description.** English speakers soften opinions far more than Spanish speakers do. *That's wrong* is grammatically perfect and lands as rude; *I'm not sure that's right* says the same thing and keeps the conversation going. This is not politeness for its own sake: a softened claim is easier to defend, because you have not committed to more than you can support.

**Reference box — softeners by type**

| Type | Words | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Modal | *might*, *may*, *could*, *would* | *That **might** be true.* |
| Frequency | *tend to*, *usually*, *generally* | *They **tend to** overcook it.* |
| Opinion frame | *I think*, *I'd say*, *it seems to me* | *I'd say it's too salty.* |
| Quantity | *a bit*, *slightly*, *a little* | *It's **a bit** dry.* |
| Negative softener | *not really*, *not exactly* | *It's **not really** my thing.* |
| Question form | *don't you think?*, *would you say…?* | *A bit heavy, don't you think?* |

*One or two per opinion. Three or more and it stops sounding careful and starts sounding evasive.*

**Examples.**
- *It's a bit rich for me, but that might just be my taste.*
- *I'd say it needs a little more salt.*
- *It's not really what I expected, to be honest.*

**Notes.**
- *A bit* before a negative adjective is the single most useful softener in spoken English: *a bit dry*, *a bit late*, *a bit expensive*.
- *To be honest* and *to be fair* soften rather than emphasise, which surprises learners.
- Never soften a fact. *The restaurant might close at eleven* when you know it does sounds evasive rather than careful.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish allows a flat statement of opinion without it sounding aggressive, so a Spanish speaker systematically under-softens in English. The literal translation of a perfectly normal Spanish opinion lands roughly two levels harsher than intended.

**Common mistakes.**
- Saying nothing at all instead of disagreeing, which is the opposite over-correction.
- Stacking: *I think maybe it could possibly be a bit…*
- Softening a fact or a number.
- *I'm not agree* — the fossilised error worth naming once.

**Mission (~35 min).** Review a meal, a restaurant or a dish someone cooked for you — and find something to criticise without being rude. Required: at least six softeners from at least four types; no opinion with three or more stacked; and at least one flat factual statement, to show you know the difference. All four core vocabulary items.

**Micro-tasks.**
- *Mon (5 min).* Write five blunt opinions. Soften each one.
- *Wed (5 min).* Take one criticism and write it three ways: soft, normal, firm. Which would you actually say?
- *Fri (5 min).* Record 90 seconds describing a meal you didn't enjoy, without being rude once.

**Self-check.**
1. Six softeners from four types?
2. Any opinion with three or more stacked?
3. Did I soften a fact by mistake?
4. Would a British listener find any sentence blunt?
5. All four core items?

**Correction prompt.**
> Focus only on hedging and softening. (1) Mark each softener and say whether it fits. (2) Mark every flat opinion that an English speaker would have softened. (3) Flag any stacking. (4) Flag any fact I softened by mistake. Do not correct anything else.

**Audio lines**
1. *It's a bit rich for me, but that might just be my taste.*
2. *I'd say it needs a little more salt.*
3. *It's not really what I expected, to be honest.*
4. *A bit heavy, don't you think?*
5. *I'm not sure that's right.*
