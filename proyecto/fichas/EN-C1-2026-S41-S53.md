## Week 41 · 5–11 Oct · EN-F11 · Summarising Complex Information
*Occurrence 1 of 4 — Compressing without distorting, for an audience without your context*

**Topic:** T09 Education & Lifelong Learning · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~40 min + 20 min

**Description.** Summarising is a mediation skill, not a comprehension skill: the test is whether your listener can now act, not whether you understood. At C1 the difficulty is selection under constraint — deciding what to drop, and being able to say what you dropped. The structural move is top-down: conclusion first, then two or three supports, then the caveat.

**Reference box — the summary skeleton**

| Slot | Function | Language |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Signal length | sets expectations | *Give me two minutes and I'll take you through it* |
| 2 · Conclusion first | the answer at the top | *The short version is…* / *If you only remember one thing…* |
| 3 · Two or three supports | the load-bearing points | *Three things came out of it. First… Second… And the one that matters…* |
| 4 · Declared omission | converts lossy into trustworthy | *I'm skipping the methodology — ask if you want it* |
| 5 · Caveat | marks the limits | *The sample was small, so treat it as directional* |
| 6 · Separate evaluation | keeps report and opinion apart | *That's what they found. What I think about it is…* |

**Examples.**
- *The short version is that the course works, but only for people who already have the basics.*
- *I'm skipping the methodology. Ask me if you want it.*
- *That's the finding. My own view is that they measured the wrong thing.*

**Notes.**
- Saying what you left out converts a lossy summary into a trustworthy one.
- Keep reporting and evaluating separate. *They found X* and *I think X is wrong* must not blur.
- The order events happened is almost never the right order to explain them.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish academic and professional prose often builds bottom-up, with the conclusion last. English expects the answer at the top. Transferring the Spanish shape makes an English summary read as evasive rather than thorough.

**Common mistakes.**
- Chronological retelling instead of a summary.
- *Resume* meaning *summarise* — false friend; it means to restart.
- Compressing by removing words rather than content: dense and unreadable.
- Losing hedging in compression, turning a tentative finding into a fact.

**Skill expressions**

## Week 42 · 12–18 Oct · EN-F13 · Hedging and Softening Claims
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Turning flat assertions into defensible ones*

**Topic:** T06 Environment & Sustainability · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~45 min + 15 min

**Description.** Hedging is argumentative precision, not politeness. A hedged claim is harder to attack, easier to revise and more acceptable to an audience that does not share your view. English does this lexically and modally — there is no hedging tense. It lives in adverbs, modals, reporting structures and impersonal constructions.

**Reference box — hedging devices by type**

| Type | Devices | What it hedges |
|---|---|---|
| Modal | *might*, *may well*, *would*, *could* | the certainty of the claim |
| Frequency / tendency | *tends to*, *typically*, *broadly*, *in most cases* | its generality |
| Evidential | *there's some evidence that*, *as far as I can tell*, *on the numbers I've seen* | your grounds for it |
| Impersonal | *it could be argued that*, *there's a case for*, *one reading is* | your ownership of it |
| Self-limiting | *I'd be careful about*, *I could be wrong*, *speaking outside my area* | your authority |
| Scope | *up to a point*, *in principle*, *at least for X* | its range of application |

**Examples.**
- *That tends to be the case, though the data is thinner than people assume.*
- *There's some evidence to suggest that offsets underperform their stated value.*
- *I'd be careful about generalising from one grid.*

**Notes.**
- *Would* is the single most productive device: *I'd say*, *I'd argue*, *I'd be careful about*.
- One or two hedges per claim is the ceiling. Stacking destroys credibility.
- Never hedge a verifiable number — it reads as evasive, not careful.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish speakers systematically under-hedge in English. *Eso no funciona* translates literally as *that doesn't work*, which lands as blunt; the equivalent English register is *I don't think that's going to work*.

**Common mistakes.**
- *Eventually* ≠ *eventualmente*. It means "in the end", not "possibly".
- *Maybe* sentence-initially reads tentative rather than considered. Prefer *perhaps* or *I'd say*.
- Under-hedging, which reads as aggressive rather than confident.

**Skill expressions**

**Mission (~45 min).** Argue a position on carbon offsetting. Required: six distinct devices from at least four types, no sentence with more than two, at least one deliberately unhedged factual statement, all four core vocabulary items.

**Micro-tasks.**

- *Wed (5 min).* Reply to an online comment you disagree with, hedging exactly once.
- *Fri (5 min).* Record 90 seconds on offsets. Count hedges: under three is stiff, over eight is evasive.

**Self-check.**
1. Six devices from four different types?
2. Any sentence with three or more hedges?
3. Did I hedge a verifiable number?
4. Is there at least one flat factual statement, to show I know the difference?
5. All four core items?

**Correction prompt.**
> Focus only on hedging. (1) Mark each device and say whether it fits the claim and the register. (2) Mark where I asserted flatly and a C1 professional would have hedged. (3) Mark over-hedging and show the tighter version. (4) Did I hedge any verifiable fact? Do not rewrite the text.

2. *There's some evidence to suggest that offsets underperform their stated value.*
3. *I'd be careful about generalising from one grid.*
4. *It could be argued that the cost falls on the wrong people.*
5. *As far as I can tell, nobody has measured this properly.*

## Week 43 · 19–25 Oct · EN-V03 · Advanced Phrasal Verbs
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — The phrasal verbs that replace formal Latinate verbs*

**Topic:** T12 Hobbies & Free Time · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~40 min + 15 min

**Description.** At C1 the issue is not knowing phrasal verbs — it is that you avoid them and reach for the Latinate synonym, which is transparent to a Spanish speaker and therefore comfortable. The result is technically correct English that sounds like a translation: *investigate* instead of *look into*, *tolerate* instead of *put up with*, *postpone* instead of *put off*. This week the Latinate options are banned.

**Reference box — separability**

| Pattern | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Separable, noun object | either position | *turn off the light* / *turn the light off* |
| Separable, pronoun object | **must** split | *turn it off*, never *turn off it* |
| Inseparable, two-part | never splits | *look after the kids* |
| Three-part | never splits | *put up with*, *come up with*, *look forward to* |
| Intransitive | no object | *give up*, *break down*, *wind down* |

**Examples.**
- *I got into it through a friend and ended up sinking about ten years into it.*
- *I've been meaning to take it back up, but I keep putting it off.*
- *It started as something to wind down with and turned into a second job.*

**Notes.**
- Register is not uniformly informal: *carry out*, *point out* and *bring about* are fully acceptable in formal writing.
- Many phrasal verbs are polysemous. *Pick up* has at least six senses; learn them by context, not by list.
- Three-part verbs are the ones learners most often mangle, because the middle particle looks droppable and is not.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish has no phrasal verbs, so a Spanish speaker's default retrieval is always the Latinate verb — which exists in English but sits one register higher. That mismatch, not error, is what makes advanced English sound stiff.

**Common mistakes.**
- *Discuss about* — no preposition. Contrast with *talk about*.
- Pronoun after the particle: *turn on it*.
- Splitting a three-part verb.
- Defaulting to the Latinate verb, which is the whole point this week.

## Week 45 · 2–8 Nov · EN-F15 · Managing a Conversation
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Taking, holding and yielding the floor; repairing without stopping*

**Topic:** T08 Relationships & Family · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 10-min conversation or 400 words · **Time:** ~40 min + 15 min

**Description.** This is probably the skill that limits spoken English most, and the least taught. Courses train you to produce sentences; they do not train you to get a turn, keep it while you think, hand it over, or recover when you lose the thread mid-sentence. Fluency is not speed — it is the ability to keep the channel open while you think.

**Reference box — the five moves**

| Move | Function | Language |
|---|---|---|
| Taking | get a turn without waiting for silence | *Can I jump in on that?* / *Sorry, before we move on —* |
| Holding | keep the turn while searching | *Give me a second, I'm working out how to say this* / *There's a word I'm looking for — bear with me* |
| Yielding | hand it back deliberately | *But you were going to say something* / *What's your take?* |
| Repairing | fix a broken sentence out loud | *Let me start that again* / *That came out wrong — what I meant was…* |
| Buying time | fill while thinking | *That's a good question, actually* / *It depends what you mean by…* |

**Examples.**
- *Sorry, before we move on — can I go back to what you said about your sister?*
- *There's a word I'm looking for, bear with me… estranged. That's it.*
- *That came out wrong. What I meant was that I understood why she did it, not that I agreed.*

**Notes.**
- Announcing that you are searching for a word is a C1 move, not a failure. Native speakers do it constantly.
- Silence costs more in English than in Spanish. Filled pauses (*right*, *so*, *I mean*) keep the turn.
- Explicit repair is far better than trailing off.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish conversation tolerates and expects more overlap and interruption than English does — but English compensates by making the *taking* explicit and verbal. Where Spanish simply starts talking, English announces first.

**Common mistakes.**
- Apologising for your English mid-conversation. It moves the listener's attention onto your level.
- *Sorry, my English is bad* — never say this. Say *bear with me*.
- Abandoning a lost sentence instead of repairing it aloud.
- Waiting for a clean gap that never comes.

| **to set boundaries** ★ | collocation | therapeutic register, now normalised | poner límites |
| **to drift apart** ★ | phrasal | without conflict | distanciarse |
| **to clear the air** ★ | idiom | | aclarar las cosas |
| **to be there for someone** ★ | chunk | hard to translate literally | estar ahí para alguien |
| to fall out with | phrasal | | pelearse con |
| to patch things up | phrasal | | reconciliarse |
| to take something the wrong way | chunk | | tomarse algo a mal |
| a sounding board | idiom | someone to think aloud with | alguien con quien pensar en voz alta |
| to walk on eggshells | idiom | | pisar huevos |
| an in-joke | noun | | chiste privado |
| to bottle something up | phrasal | | guardarse algo |
| tough love | collocation | | cariño exigente |
| to grow apart | phrasal | ≠ *grow up* | distanciarse con los años |
| a support network | collocation | | red de apoyo |

**Skill expressions**

| Expression | Function | ES |
|---|---|---|
| *Can I jump in?* | takes the floor | ¿puedo meter una? |
| *Sorry, before we move on —* | takes it back to an earlier point | perdón, antes de seguir… |

**Mission.** Have a real conversation of at least ten minutes in English about something personal. Required: take the floor twice, hold it once while visibly searching for a word, repair one broken sentence aloud, yield deliberately twice, all four core vocabulary items. Evidence: a recording, or 400 words reconstructing what you said and what worked.

## Week 46 · 9–15 Nov · EN-G16 · Inversion, Cleft and Emphatic Structures
*Occurrence 1 of 2 — Moving emphasis without raising your voice*

**Topic:** T05 Travel & Culture Shock · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~45 min + 20 min

**Description.** English word order is rigid, so shifting emphasis requires structural moves rather than the free reordering Spanish allows. These structures are the clearest single marker separating C1 writing from B2 writing, and the most reliably absent from advanced learners' output.

**Reference box — the four families**

| Family | Pattern | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative inversion | *Not until / Only after / Rarely / Never / Little* + **aux + subject** | *Not until I'd lived there did I understand.* | auxiliary compulsory |
| *Wh-* cleft | *What… is/was…* | *What struck me was the silence.* | most natural in speech |
| *It-* cleft | *It was X that/who…* | *It was the bureaucracy that broke me.* | more written |
| Fronting | object or complement first | *That I'll never forget.* | marked; use sparingly |
| *All/The thing* cleft | *All I wanted was…* / *The thing that got me was…* | | informal, very common |

**Examples.**
- *Not until I'd lived there a year did I understand why nobody complains.*
- *What struck me most wasn't the language — it was the silence on trains.*
- *It was the bureaucracy, not the weather, that nearly broke me.*
- *All I wanted was somebody to explain the recycling.*

**Notes.**
- Negative inversion requires auxiliary inversion: *Not only did I…*, never *Not only I did…*.
- These structures are marked. Two or three in a text is stylish; six is theatrical.
- The *All I wanted was* pattern is the one to start with: informal, frequent, and it teaches the cleft logic without the formal register.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish moves emphasis by moving words (*a mí no me gustó*). English cannot, so it builds a structure instead. Every Spanish emphatic word-order shift needs a cleft or an inversion in English, not a word-order change.

**Common mistakes.**
- *Never I have seen* instead of *Never have I seen*.
- *It was then when…* — should be *It was then that…*.
- Using inversion where plain order carries the same weight, so the emphasis reads as unearned.

| a layover / stopover | term | | escala |
| to travel light | collocation | | viajar ligero |

**Skill expressions**

| Expression | Function | ES |
|---|---|---|

| *Only after… did…* | same, with a time focus | solo después de… |

| *It was X that…* | isolates the cause | fue X lo que… |
| *All I wanted was…* | reduces to the essential | lo único que quería era… |

**Mission (~45 min).** Write about a moment of culture shock you actually had. Required: two negative inversions, two clefts of different types, one fronting, no more than six marked structures in total, all four core vocabulary items.

**Micro-tasks.**
- *Mon (5 min).* Take five flat sentences about your life abroad and rewrite each as a cleft.
- *Wed (5 min).* Write three negative inversions. Check the auxiliary every time.
- *Fri (5 min).* Record 90 seconds about something that surprised you on arrival, using at least two clefts.

**Self-check.**
1. Is the auxiliary present in every inversion?
2. Two different cleft types, or the same one twice?
3. More than six marked structures?
4. Does each one earn its emphasis, or is it decoration?
5. All four core items?

**Correction prompt.**

## Week 47 · 16–22 Nov · EN-F14 · Giving Constructive Feedback
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Saying something difficult so that it can be acted on*

**Topic:** T01 Work & Career · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~40 min + 15 min

**Description.** Feedback in English professional culture follows conventions strong enough to learn as formulas. The core one: describe behaviour and impact, not character. *You were unprepared* is character; *the deck arrived an hour before the meeting, so we spent fifteen minutes reading* is behaviour and impact — and only the second gives the person something to change.

**Reference box — the four slots**

| Slot | Function | Language |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Permission | signals it is coming | *Can I give you some feedback on the report?* |
| 2 · Behaviour | observable, not interpreted | *When the estimate changed without a note…* |
| 3 · Impact | consequence, not judgement | *…the client assumed we'd hidden it.* |
| 4 · Invitation | hands the turn back | *What's your read on why it slipped?* |
| (optional) Limit | your own uncertainty | *I might be missing context here.* |

**Examples.**
- *Can I give you some feedback on the report? Two things worked, one didn't.*
- *When the estimate changed without a note, the client assumed we'd hidden it. That's the bit I'd do differently.*
- *I might be missing context — what's your read on why it slipped?*

**Notes.**
- Asking permission first is near-mandatory in most English-speaking workplaces.
- The praise-criticism-praise sandwich is now widely disliked: people hear only the praise, or wait for the middle.
- Naming your own uncertainty makes the feedback easier to accept without weakening it.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish workplace feedback tends to be either indirect to the point of invisibility or blunt and personal. English has a well-defined middle register for which Spanish lacks a fixed formula — which is why learning the four slots is worth more here than learning vocabulary.

**Common mistakes.**
- *You always* / *You never* — indefensible, and guarantees defensiveness.
- Passive evasion: *mistakes were made*.
- Softening until the message is invisible. If they leave unsure there was a problem, there wasn't one.
- Feedback on personality rather than output.

## Week 49 · 30 Nov–6 Dec · EN-V05 · Professional and Academic Collocations
*Occurrence 1 of 2 — The verb-noun pairs native speakers never choose consciously*

**Topic:** T02 Personal Finance & Banking · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~45 min + 20 min

**Description.** Collocation is where advanced learners are exposed most reliably. Grammar correct, vocabulary wide, and then they *make* a decision instead of *taking* one, or *do* research instead of *conducting* it. There is no rule; the pairings are conventional and must be learned as pairs. It is the highest-return vocabulary work at C1, because the individual words are already known.

**Reference box — high-frequency verb-noun sets**

| Noun family | Conventional verb |
|---|---|
| conclusion, inference | **draw**, **reach** |

| concern, objection, question | **raise** |
| requirement, standard, deadline | **meet** |
| condition, criterion | **satisfy** |
| obligation, commitment | **fulfil**, **honour** |
| loan, policy, mortgage | **take out** |
| risk, cost, loss | **bear**, **shoulder**, **absorb** |

## Week 50 · 7–13 Dec · EN-F12 · Persuading and Negotiating
*Occurrence 1 of 2 — Moving someone's position without pushing*

**Topic:** T07 Housing & City Life · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~45 min + 15 min

**Description.** Persuasion in English leans on framing rather than force: presenting your proposal in terms of the other side's interest, offering conditional concessions, making it easy to say yes. The move that matters most is separating positions from interests — a position has one solution, an interest has several.

**Reference box — the negotiation toolkit**

| Move | Function | Language |
|---|---|---|
| Anchor | sets the reference point | *I was thinking somewhere around X* |
| Conditional offer | concedes nothing unilaterally | *If you could be flexible on the date, I'd be able to move on the price* |
| Interest probe | finds the real need | *What would it take for this to work for you?* |
| Reframe to interest | converts a position into a problem | *I think we want the same thing; we disagree about how* |
| Trial close | lowers the commitment | *Suppose we tried it for three months and reviewed it* |
| Limited refusal | says no and offers something | *I can't do X, but here's what I can do* |
| Reason-giving | raises compliance measurably | *…because the alternative is another month empty* |

**Examples.**
- *If you could be flexible on the date, I'd be able to move on the price.*
- *What would it take for this to work for you?*
- *I can't do the full amount, but I can do it in two payments this month.*

**Notes.**
- Conditional offers concede nothing until reciprocated. That is why they are the workhorse.
- *What would it take?* is the most useful negotiating question in English.
- Giving a reason materially increases compliance, even a weak one. *Because* does real work.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish negotiation often opens closer to the real number; English expects an anchor and reads an immediately reasonable offer as the ceiling rather than the midpoint.

**Common mistakes.**
- Opening with your minimum.
- *I want* repeated — replace with *I'm looking for*, *what would help is*, *my constraint is*.
- Conceding without a counter-ask, which resets expectations for the whole negotiation.
- Confusing persuasion with volume or repetition.

## Week 51 · 14–20 Dec · EN-G15 · Reduced Relative and Participle Clauses
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Compressing written English without losing clarity*

**Topic:** T04 Health & Wellbeing · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~45 min + 20 min

**Description.** This is a structural difference between B2 and C1 written English. *The patients who were given the drug* becomes *the patients given the drug*. The information is identical; the density is not. The rule is narrow and learnable: drop the relative pronoun and the auxiliary when the clause is passive or continuous, and only when the pronoun is the **subject**.

**Reference box — what reduces to what**

| Full clause | Reduced | Type |
|---|---|---|
| *the study which was published…* | *the study published…* | past participle (passive) |
| *anyone who is taking…* | *anyone taking…* | present participle (active) |
| *because it had been delayed…* | *having been delayed…* | perfect passive |
| *after she had finished…* | *having finished…* | perfect active |
| *the data which is available* | *the data available* | adjective postposition |
| *the book which I read* | ✗ cannot reduce | pronoun is the object |

**Examples.**
- *The study, published last month, contradicts three earlier trials.*
- *Anyone taking that medication should avoid alcohol.*
- *Having been misdiagnosed twice, she stopped trusting the referral system.*

**Notes.**
- Present participle = active; past participle = passive. Getting this backwards is the classic error.
- Dangling participles are common and real: the participle's subject must be the main clause's subject.
- These are written structures. In conversation they sound stiff.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish has gerund and participle clauses too, but tolerates a dangling subject far more readily (*estando en Madrid, me robaron*). English does not: the same construction reads as an error.

**Common mistakes.**
- *Walking into the room, the lights were off* — the lights were not walking.
- Reducing where the pronoun is the object: *the book read*.

## Week 52 · 21–27 Dec · EN-V08 · Precision: Avoiding Overused Words
*Occurrence 1 of 3 — Replacing the ten words you overuse*

**Topic:** T10 Food & Cooking · **Type:** open production · **Evidence:** 400 words or 6 min · **Time:** ~40 min + 15 min

**Description.** Advanced English is limited less by what the speaker does not know than by what they reach for first. *Very*, *good*, *bad*, *thing*, *a lot*, *nice*, *interesting*, *make*, *get* and *big* cover an enormous amount of ground badly. **Reference box — the ten banned words and where to go instead**

| Banned | Ask instead |
|---|---|
| very | Is there one stronger adjective? *very tired* → *exhausted* |

## Week 53 · 28 Dec–3 Jan · EN-F17 · Register Shifting
*Occurrence 1 of 2 — The same content, three audiences, three registers*

**Topic:** T03 Technology & AI · **Type:** adaptive production · **Evidence:** 3 versions, ~150 words each · **Time:** ~50 min + 15 min

**Description.** Register is the C1 skill most likely to be invisible to the speaker and obvious to everyone else. Writing to a regulator the way you would write to a colleague is the commonest way advanced speakers misfire. The content is not wrong; the packaging is. Register moves along several axes at once, and the failure is almost always mixing them within one text.

**Reference box — the six axes**

| Axis | Informal → Formal |
|---|---|
| Lexis | Germanic (*get*, *put*, *let*) → Latinate (*obtain*, *place*, *permit*) |
| Structure | Verbal (*we tested it*) → Nominal (*testing was carried out*) |
| Person | *I / we* → impersonal (*it was found that*) |
| Hedging | sparse → dense |
| Contractions | *don't*, *it's* → *do not*, *it is* |
| Phrasal verbs | *find out* → *ascertain* |

**Examples.** The same message, three registers:
- To a colleague: *This won't scale and we both know it.*
- To a manager: *I've got concerns about how this behaves at volume — worth a conversation before we commit.*
- In a formal report: *Scalability has not been demonstrated at the volumes anticipated, and further assessment is recommended prior to commitment.*

**Notes.**
- Formal English is not harder English; it is a different set.
- Over-formalising email reads as cold or sarcastic in most English-speaking workplaces.
- Nominalisation raises formality and lowers readability. Use it deliberately, not by default.

**Contrast with Spanish.** Spanish marks formality heavily through address (*tú/usted*) and verb morphology, which English lacks entirely. English carries the whole load lexically and structurally — so a Spanish speaker who gets the politeness right can still get the register badly wrong.

**Common mistakes.**
- Equating formality with difficulty.
- Register mixing within a paragraph — the commonest real error.
- Latinate vocabulary in a message meant to be warm.
